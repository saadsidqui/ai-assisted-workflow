# Pedagogy for this program

The audience is mixed and mostly not technical. The presenter is new to teaching and speaks slowly. Every choice below makes the lesson easier to follow for the room, or easier to deliver for the presenter.

## Sequence

The order of every lesson is outcome, concept, example, checkpoint, practice, recap, as set in `DESIGN.md` section 8. Inside a unit, the example comes first and the concept is named afterwards. People understand a rule faster when they have just seen a case of it.

## One idea at a time

- One idea per slide, and one idea per paragraph of learner text.
- A unit that needs two ideas becomes two units.
- Before adding a slide, ask what the room will be able to do after it that they could not do before. If the answer is nothing new, cut the slide.

## Concrete before abstract

- Every concept is introduced through something people recognize from work: an email, a request to an assistant, a meeting note, a short code change, depending on the track.
- Use the running example of the session (the fictional engagement) wherever it fits. Switching examples costs the presenter time and the room attention.
- Define each term at its first use, in plain French, with the glossary's rendering. Never assume the audience knows the jargon.

## Checkpoints

- **Question:** exactly as the card states it.
- **Pause:** the presenter counts slowly to seven before saying anything. Silence is part of the method.
- **Answers:** take two or three from the room.
- **Recap:** close with one sentence.
- **Silence:** if nobody answers, rephrase the question once, then offer one possible answer and ask whether the room agrees.
- **A question that goes deeper than the session** goes on the parking lot and gets a written answer later (S-01).

## Retrieval rather than repetition

- A recap asks the room first ("What do you keep from this?") and only then shows the statements.
- Remembering takes effort, and the effort is what makes it stick.
- A recap never repeats an earlier slide word for word. It ties each point back to its stop on the route and to the running example.
- Mixing related questions in practice (spacing and interleaving) helps more than drilling one point.

(The notes on retrieval, spacing and interleaving are paraphrased, with credit, from the `teach` skill in mattpocock/skills, MIT, commit `4ae2317ddb22b046e26455e4f9c9b3aaaf62212d`. No text is copied.)

## Practice that reveals something

- A task is short and bounded, on synthetic material, with a stated expected result.
- Where the card allows it, the material contains something that verification will catch, the way S-02's planted trap does. Finding it teaches review better than a slide does.
- Never rescue a weak first draft during practice: the weak draft is the teaching material.

## Tiers and time

- Only required topics are taught in a session. Recommended and optional topics are reference reading.
- Write for the planned minutes, not the booking. The margin belongs to the slow pace and the questions.
- Mark what the presenter can skip when late, following the cut order in S-01 for core modules.
